Agentic Customer 360 — Proactive Intervention Desk
1. PROBLEM STATEMENT DESCRIPTION
1.1 The Domain and The Status Quo
“Customer 360” has long been the holy grail of enterprise architecture: the goal of aggregating a single, unified 
view of a customer’s entire footprint across a business. Historically, large organizations have attempted to 
solve this by dumping data into massive lakes and building complex CRM dashboards. However, a fundamental 
problem remains: unifying data does not equate to acting on it. Dashboards are passive. They rely on a human 
account manager or support rep to log in, synthesize the information, and make a judgment call. When dealing 
with thousands or millions of customers, it is impossible for humans to continuously monitor these unified profiles 
to catch subtle, compounding shifts in behavior — like a high-value client quietly building up churn risk, or a 
missed window for a strategic upsell.
1.2 The Agentic AI Solution and The Data Influx
Agentic AI offers a paradigm shift from passive observation to continuous, autonomous reasoning. Modern 
customers generate a relentless, asynchronous influx of multi-modal data: support tickets, e-commerce trans -
actions, travel histories, daily spends, product telemetry events, and communication transcripts. Instead of 
merely plotting these disconnected data points on a chart, an agentic system can continuously parse this chaotic 
stream, separating signal from noise, and synthesizing a coherent narrative of the customer’s current state.
1.3 Ambient Agents vs. Prompt-Based Chatbots
Crucially, this solution is not a chatbot. Traditional generative AI relies on a prompt-based paradigm — it sits 
dormant until a human asks it a question. For Customer 360, we require ambient agents. These are living, 
background processes that do not wait for human initiation. They are triggered asynchronously by environmental 
changes: an event-driven trigger (e.g., a dropped transaction or a furious support ticket) or a timely scheduled 
occurrence (e.g., a 30-day account health check).
To achieve this, these ambient agents must utilize sophisticated memory architectures. By maintaining both 
short-term working memory (recent interactions) and long-term memory (historical baselines, past interventions, 
and life events), these agents build an evolving state for each customer. When a new event occurs, the ambient 
agent doesn’t just evaluate it in a vacuum; it uses its memory to contextualize the event, proactively reacting to 
the environment to flag risks or initiate actions before a human even knows to look.
VERY IMPORT ANT: This is a genuinely unfamiliar problem space for most teams — production-grade patterns 
for agent memory, MAS coordination, and streaming RAG freshness aren’t things most people have built intuition 
for through everyday coding experience. That makes reading how others have approached these problems 
(papers, engineering blogs, conference talks) less of an optional nicety here and more of a load-bearing part 
of the work: the architectural choices later in this document will only hold up under real conditions if they’re 
grounded in something more than guesswork.
2. NON-NEGOTIABLE KEY COMPONENTS
As Agentic AI transitions from experimental sandboxes to live, mission-critical enterprise environments, trust and 
safety become the primary bottlenecks to adoption. Unlike traditional deterministic software, agentic systems 
are inherently non-deterministic and can produce unpredictable, compounding sequences of actions. To safely 
deploy autonomous agents that interact with customer data and financial levers, the following components are 
absolute prerequisites for any upcoming agentic architecture:
Explainability: If an agent makes a decision, there must exist proper, reproducible reasons and clear decision 
paths that articulate how it arrived at that conclusion. Black-box outputs are unacceptable in enterprise environ-
ments; the system must be able to cite its sources and logic.

Situation
The Action Agent decides to offer a high-value customer a substantial 500 retention credit. Before approving 
the action, the human-in-the-loop requires context. The agent provides a clear explanation: it cites a 40% 
drop in platform login frequency over the last three weeks (flagged by the Usage Agent) combined with a 
highly negative sentiment score on a recent, unresolved support ticket (flagged by the Support Agent).
Traceability: Every single action taken by the agent must be meticulously logged. This includes all API requests, 
internal tool calls, database queries, and RAG retrievals. This transparent audit trail is vital for post-mortems 
and debugging; it allows engineers to see exactly how the agent interacted with the environment at any given 
millisecond, making it much easier to figure out where things went wrong if they do.
Situation
An agent inappropriately flags a healthy, long-term customer as a “High Churn Risk” and spams the 
account manager with urgent alerts. Through traceability, the engineering team reviews the logs and 
discovers that during a retrieval step, the agent fetched a legally dense contract termination notice for a 
different customer with a similar name, poisoning its context window. The detailed log instantly isolates the 
bug to the entity resolution layer rather than a flaw in the agent’s core reasoning.
Guardrails: These are hard-coded, deterministic rules and bypasses that override the agent’s autonomy in 
extreme or high-risk scenarios. Because LLMs can occasionally hallucinate or fail to grasp severe real-world 
nuance, guardrails act as the ultimate safety net, ensuring that catastrophic, non-compliant, or wildly expensive 
actions are physically impossible for the agent to execute.
Situation
A customer sends a furious, legally threatening email demanding account cancellation and a full refund. 
The Synthesis Agent might try to be “helpful” by attempting to negotiate or offering a standard 10% 
discount. A hard-coded guardrail — configured to trigger on high-risk keywords like “lawsuit,” “legal 
action,” or “attorney” — instantly bypasses the agent’s standard logic, halts all autonomous outbound 
communication, and directly routes the ticket to a specialized legal escalation queue.
Human-in-the-Loop (HITL): No matter how well-reasoned an agent’s output is, autonomy without a checkpoint 
is a liability the moment real money, real relationships, or real regulatory exposure is on the line. HITL is not a 
failsafe bolted on for optics — it’s the mechanism that lets the system act with speed on low-stakes decisions 
while reserving judgment for a human wherever the cost of being wrong is high, the situation is ambiguous, or 
the action is irreversible. Just as important, HITL is where the system earns trust over time: every approval or 
override a human gives becomes a data point the system can eventually learn from, tightening or loosening its 
own autonomy boundaries.

3. WHAT TO ARRIVE AT (THE END GOAL)
It’s easy for a problem statement like this to drift into “build something that watches customers and surfaces 
insights” — and that’s precisely the trap to avoid. A system that only monitors and flags is not gradeable, because 
there’s no terminal, falsifiable output to check it against. The system must be accountable for a specific, 
committed decision, not a general sense of awareness.
Concretely, the end goal is: given a customer’s live, evolving signal set, the system must decide whether 
to trigger an intervention, and which one — from a bounded set of real, costed actions such as:
• No action — signals don’t cross a meaningful threshold; the system explicitly decides not to act, and that 
decision should be as traceable as an active one.
• Proactive retention outreach  — a discount, fee waiver, or loyalty gesture, sized appropriately to the 
customer’s value and the severity of the risk.
• Relationship-manager escalation — a human-initiated call or meeting, used when the situation is too 
nuanced or high-value for an automated message to be appropriate.
• Personalized offer — a pre-approved product (loan, higher credit line, investment product) surfaced at a 
moment inferred to be relevant, subject to eligibility rules.
• Support/service intervention — proactively reaching out to resolve a brewing issue before the customer 
has to escalate it themselves.
• Compliance/fraud hold — freezing or flagging an action pending human review, when the signal pattern 
looks like fraud, AML risk, or account takeover.
Alongside this bounded action set, there is a second, standing goal that runs underneath every decision the 
system makes: the system must maintain an accurate, continuously updated understanding of what life 
event the customer is currently going through, inferred from the pattern of transactions and actions on 
their account — a new child, a marriage, a promotion, a home purchase, financial distress, retirement, and so 
on. This inferred “current life phase” is not a one-off classification to be computed and forgotten; it belongs in 
the sophisticated memory architecture the participants design, alongside the customer’s episodic history, and it 
should be the thing multiple agents read from rather than something each agent tries to re-derive independently 
every time. Concretely: a life-event inference made by the swarm/synthesis layer today should still be available 
and correctly weighted weeks later when the Offer Agent is deciding whether a personalized offer is relevant, 
or when the Retention Agent is deciding how to interpret a new signal in light of what’s already known about this 
customer’s situation. A system that infers “new parent” correctly on day one but doesn’t carry that understanding 
forward — and instead pitches an unrelated generic offer three weeks later — has not actually met this goal, 
even if the initial inference was right.
A strong submission should be able to state, for any given moment in a customer’s timeline: what specific action 
(or explicit no-action) did the system decide on, why, and — where ground truth exists — was it the right call.
4. EXPECTED ARCHITECTURE
4.1 Agent Memory
No single agent in this system should be a stateless function that evaluates one event in isolation — the whole 
premise of “ambient” agents is that they carry context forward. That means memory needs a deliberate design, 
not just a vector database bolted on as an afterthought.
Types of memory each agent can reason about: (not a strict requirement but more of an inspiration)

• Working memory (short-term): the live, evolving state of a single customer right now — the last few events, 
the current open case, what other agents have already concluded in this session. This is cheap to write and 
read, and gets discarded or compressed once the current situation resolves.
• Episodic memory (long-term, per-customer):  a running history of what’s happened to this specific 
customer over time and what was done about it — past interventions, past flags, past outcomes (“last time 
usage dropped like this, a proactive call prevented churn; a discount email was ignored”). This is what lets 
the system get smarter about this customer rather than treating every event as the first one.
• Semantic / shared memory (cross-customer, cross-agent): general patterns and policies that aren’t tied 
to one customer — what a “typical” pre-churn usage pattern looks like across the whole book of customers, 
current offer eligibility rules, current compliance policy. This is closer to a shared knowledge base than a 
personal diary, and multiple agents read from it without needing to “own” it.
How memory is shared between agents: Rather than every agent maintaining its own private, siloed memory 
(which risks agents disagreeing on facts or duplicating retrieval work), the recommended pattern is a shared 
per-customer state board — a structured, continuously updated record that every agent can write findings to 
and read from, scoped strictly to one customer/account ID. An agent doesn’t get raw access to another agent’s 
internal reasoning trace, only to the structured conclusion it published to the board (e.g., the Usage Agent 
publishes login_frequency_trend: -40%, confidence: high , not its full chain-of-thought). This keeps handoffs 
clean and prevents context pollution, while still letting downstream agents (like a Synthesis Agent) build a 
coherent picture.
Open design questions participants must answer, not assume:
• When does an agent decide a past episodic memory is relevant enough to retrieve for the current situation, 
versus irrelevant noise from an unrelated past incident?
• How does the system prevent memory leakage or bleed-through between customers, especially at scale 
with thousands of concurrent per-customer memory stores?
• Who is responsible for memory decay/expiry — should a churn-risk flag from 18 months ago still influence 
a decision today?
4.2 Multi-Agent System (MAS) Design
Agents in this system don’t all talk to each other in the same way at every stage — different points in the pipeline 
call for different coordination patterns. Participants should deliberately choose (and justify) which pattern fits 
which stage, rather than defaulting to one topology everywhere. This is exactly the kind of decision that should 
be informed by existing research and case studies on multi-agent coordination, not picked arbitrarily — there is 
real, documented experience out there on where each pattern tends to succeed or break down, and it’s worth 
reading before committing to one.
• Handoffs (sequential): One agent finishes its work and passes a defined, scoped package of context to 
the next agent in the chain, without needing to loop back. Best for: linear pipelines where each stage is a 
genuinely separate concern — e.g., Usage Agent → Synthesis Agent → Action Agent. Advantage: simple, 
predictable, easy to trace. Disadvantage: no opportunity to catch an error introduced earlier in the chain; a 
bad handoff early on silently corrupts everything downstream.
• Swarm (parallel, independent): Multiple agents work on different slices of the same problem simultane -
ously and independently (e.g., Usage Agent, Support Agent, Transaction Agent all watching the same 
customer at once), writing their findings to the shared state board without waiting on each other. Best for: the 
initial signal-gathering stage, where different data sources genuinely don’t depend on each other. Advantage: 
fast, scales horizontally. Disadvantage: someone downstream still has to reconcile disagreement between 
swarm members — the swarm itself doesn’t resolve conflicting reads.

• Round robin: Agents take turns contributing to a shared draft or decision in a fixed sequence, each building 
on or revising the previous agent’s contribution. Best for: iterative refinement tasks like drafting a personalized 
outreach message, where tone, factual grounding, and compliance checks each need a pass. Advantage: 
every concern gets a dedicated pass. Disadvantage: slower than parallel approaches; ordering matters and 
a bad early pass can bias later ones.
• Critique-Refiner: One agent proposes an output, a second (often with a different system prompt or persona) 
critiques it against a rubric or policy, and the first agent revises. Best for: anything going in front of a human 
or a customer — e.g., a proposed retention offer gets critiqued for cost-feasibility and compliance before 
it reaches HITL. Advantage: meaningfully reduces low-quality or non-compliant outputs reaching a human. 
Disadvantage: adds latency; not suitable for anything needing sub-second response.
• Agent Debate: Two or more agents holding genuinely different perspectives argue a case explicitly, with 
their disagreement made visible rather than silently averaged away. Best for: situations where reasonable 
agents could disagree and the disagreement itself is informative — e.g., a Churn-Risk Agent says “high risk, 
offer a discount,” an Upsell Agent says “this customer is actually a growth opportunity, don’t undersell them” 
— the debate output (“both are true — this is a customer to invest in, not discount”) is more useful than either 
agent’s answer alone. Advantage: surfaces genuine ambiguity instead of hiding it. Disadvantage: needs a 
defined resolution mechanism (who breaks the tie — another agent, a rule, or the human?), or it just produces 
an unresolved argument.
A realistic system will combine these: swarm for signal-gathering, handoff into a synthesis stage, debate 
when swarm agents disagree, round robin for drafting a customer-facing message, and critique-refiner as a 
final quality/compliance pass before anything reaches HITL.
4.3 Ambient Agents and Trigger Types
Every agent in this system is “ambient” — none of them wait for a human to open a chat window and ask a 
question. But not all triggers work the same way, and participants should be explicit about which type governs 
each agent:
• Time-based (scheduled) triggers: the agent runs on a fixed cadence regardless of events — e.g., a 30-
day account health check, a quarterly KYC re-verification, a nightly rollup of the past day’s transactions. 
These catch slow-building patterns that no single event would surface.
• Event-based (reactive) triggers: the agent fires the moment a specific event arrives in the stream — a new 
support ticket, a large transaction, a failed login. These are the fast-path triggers, and are what most of the 
“real-time” feel of the system depends on.
• Agent-dependent (derived) triggers: the agent fires not because of a raw external event, but because 
another agent published a finding that crosses some threshold — e.g., the Life-Event Inference Agent only 
activates once both the Transaction Agent (large deposit) and the Usage Agent (spike in home-loan related 
app searches) have independently flagged something in the same time window. This is what lets the system 
correlate weak signals into a strong one, instead of treating every raw event as independently actionable.
A well-designed system will use all three: time-based for baseline hygiene, event-based for responsiveness, and 
agent-dependent for the correlation logic that’s the actual point of having multiple agents in the first place.

6. PRODUCTION BAR CHECKLIST
A demo that works once, on a clean happy-path run, is not the same as a system built to a production bar. This 
checklist is what separates the two — participants should be able to point to each item below and show, not just 
claim, that it’s handled.
6.1 Streaming Ingestion & Live Retrieval
•  Continuous ingestion pipeline that accepts an ongoing, out-of-order, multi-source event stream (not a 
one-shot batch upload) — e.g., transactions, support tickets, app events, KYC updates all arriving asynchro-
nously and at different velocities.
•  Transforms applied on the live stream , not just at query time. For Customer 360, examples include: 
computing a rolling 30-day login-frequency average as new usage events arrive; joining a new transaction 
against the customer’s historical spend baseline to compute a live anomaly score; enriching an incoming 
support ticket with a sentiment score the moment it lands; maintaining a windowed aggregation of transaction 
volume per account to catch a spike within seconds of it happening.
•  Vector DB / index kept live, not rebuilt from scratch — when a new support ticket, policy document, or 
CRM note arrives, it should become retrievable without a full re-index, and stale embeddings should not 
silently persist past the point they’re outdated.
•  Correct handling of late or out-of-order events — a transaction or ticket that arrives out of sequence 
shouldn’t corrupt a windowed aggregation or double-count in a rollup.
6.2 Multi-Agent Coordination
•  A working orchestration layer  — whether orchestrator-workers, swarm, or a hybrid — where agents 
genuinely specialize (different tools, different data access, different prompts) rather than being copies of the 
same generalist agent with a different label.
•  Explicit handoffs where context passed from one agent to the next is deliberately scoped (structured 
findings, not a full context dump), and this scoping decision is documented and justified.
•  Demonstrated coordination under disagreement — at least one scenario where two agents produce 
conflicting reads on the same customer, and the system has a defined mechanism (debate, critique-refiner, 
or an arbitration rule) to resolve it rather than silently picking one.
6.3 Human-in-the-Loop
•  A real approval checkpoint before any customer-facing or costly action — not a log line claiming “human 
approved,” but an actual interruption in the flow where a human can approve, reject, or modify the proposed 
action.
•  Escalation for ambiguity, not just for cost — if the system’s own confidence is low, or two agents disagree 
and can’t resolve it, that should also route to a human, independent of the dollar amount involved.
•  An audit-ready approval log — every HITL decision (approved, rejected, modified) is recorded with the 
context that was shown to the human at the time, so a reviewer can later reconstruct exactly what the human 
saw and decided.
•  A path for the human to ask “why” — the human approver isn’t just shown a recommendation, they can 
query the system for its reasoning and source citations before deciding.

6.4 Guardrails
•  Deterministic, hard-coded stop conditions that cannot be reasoned around by the LLM — e.g., a legal-
threat keyword match halts autonomous outbound communication regardless of what any agent “decides.”
•  PII protection — customer-identifying data (account numbers, SSNs/national IDs, full transaction details) 
is masked, tokenized, or redacted wherever it isn’t strictly necessary for the current agent’s task, including 
in logs and in any prompt sent to an LLM.
•  Role-based information access enforced at the data layer, not just the prompt layer — an agent that 
isn’t supposed to see trading/brokerage data, for instance, should be structurally unable to query it, not 
merely instructed not to. A prompt-level instruction (“don’t look at X”) is not a guardrail.
•  Safe tool-calling boundaries — every tool an agent can invoke has a defined blast radius (e.g., an agent 
can draft a retention offer but cannot send it or apply a discount to the account without passing through HITL).
6.5 Observability, Traceability & Explainability
•  Full trace logging — every agent invocation, tool call, retrieval, and handoff is logged with enough detail 
to reconstruct the full decision path after the fact.
•  Metrics, not just logs — latency per stage (event time → decision time → action time), agent-level confi-
dence scores, HITL approval/override rates, and false-positive/false-negative rates against any available 
ground truth.
•  Prompt and tool-call history retained per decision  — for any final action, it should be possible to pull 
up exactly which prompts were sent, which tools were called, and what each returned, not just the final 
summary.
•  Explainability as a first-class output, not a bolt-on — every recommended or executed action ships with 
a citation-backed explanation (which signals, which memory, which source records) generated as part of the 
decision process itself, not reconstructed after the fact by asking the model to “explain what you just did.”

7. DELIVERABLES
7 .1 Research
A documented, running list of every paper, engineering blog, technical write-up, or video the participant 
consulted to understand this domain — with a short note per item on what was taken from it and which specific 
decision in the final system it informed. This isn’t a bibliography for its own sake: it’s evidence that architectural 
choices (which MAS pattern was used where, how memory was scoped, how retrieval freshness was handled) 
were arrived at deliberately, not defaulted into. Given how new and fragmented the practical literature on agentic 
systems still is, this domain genuinely rewards deep, deliberate reading over trial-and-error — the research log 
is where that has to show.
7 .2 Codebase
• AI usage: Using AI tools to help implement your own idea, or to brainstorm and explore options, is not 
discouraged — that’s a normal and expected part of how this kind of system gets built today. What is strongly 
discouraged is outsourcing the actual decisions to AI — which MAS topology to use, how to scope memory, 
what the trigger logic should be. Those calls should be the participant’s own, informed by the research above; 
AI is a tool for execution and ideation here, not a substitute for the participant’s own judgment.
• Frontend expectations: No flashy frontend is expected or rewarded. What is expected is a bare-minimum, 
working way to actually see the system doing its job — the influx of data arriving, agents acting on it, and the 
system arriving at (and explaining) its final decisions. A plain terminal log, a simple table view, or a minimal 
dashboard is entirely sufficient, as long as it’s real and reflects what the system is actually doing, not a 
mocked-up illustration of it.
• Integration: All components — ingestion, agents, memory, retrieval, HITL, guardrails — must be stitched 
together and genuinely working end-to-end, not a collection of disconnected pieces that each work in 
isolation. A reviewer should be able to run the system and watch data go in one end and a decision come 
out the other.
• Code structure: The codebase should have clearly separated components for each agent, with each agent’s 
prompt, tool definitions, and input/output schemas cleanly and explicitly structured — not buried inline or 
scattered across the codebase. A reviewer should be able to open the code and immediately identify what 
each agent is, what it can do, and what shape of data it expects and returns.
7 .3 System Architecture Diagram
A cleanly drawn system architecture diagram that illustrates:
• Every component in the system — each agent, each data source, memory stores, the retrieval layer, HITL 
checkpoints, and guardrails.
• Where each component’s data or input comes from, and what output it produces.
• Handoffs between agents shown explicitly and directionally — not a cloud of boxes with arrows everywhere, 
but a clear trace of “this agent hands this specific package of context to that agent.”
• Each agent’s toolset shown clearly, including what data source each tool reads from or writes to — a tool 
listed without a stated data source is an incomplete diagram.
• A visual distinction between synchronous flows (things that must happen immediately, like an authorization-
time fraud check) and asynchronous flows (things that can happen on a schedule or in the background).
7 .4 Solution Document (3 Pages)
A concise, 3-page document explaining the solution: the problem being solved, the architecture and why it was 
designed that way, the key decisions made (and the research or reasoning behind them), and known limitations 
or tradeoffs. Three pages is a hard constraint, not a suggestion — the ability to explain a complex agentic system 
clearly and concisely is itself part of what’s being evaluated.

7 .5 Testing & Evaluation
Results of testing and evaluation against the defined test scenarios — including what the system got right, what

8. EVALUATION CRITERIA
8.1 How Scenarios Will Be Evaluated
For each scenario, participants will be given the live event stream (replayed as described in the architecture 
sections above) — but they will not be given the hidden ground-truth answer key. What they will be given is a 
defined inferred-events schema: a required structure for how their system must log its own outputs over time 
— the inferred customer state at each point, the confidence level, and the action decided on (or explicit no-
action), each timestamped.
It is the participants’ responsibility to ensure their system actually writes out this inferred-events file in the 
specified schema as it processes the live stream — this is not an optional logging nicety, it is the artifact 
evaluators will score against the hidden ground truth. A system that reasons well internally but doesn’t emit its 
conclusions in the required schema cannot be evaluated, and will be scored accordingly.
Brownie points will be awarded to participants that go a step further and build their own small scoring harness — 
a script or tool that takes their inferred-events output and a sample ground-truth file (from the practice scenarios, 
where this will be made available) and computes how well their system’s inferences and actions line up with the 
known-correct answers. This is not required, but it demonstrates exactly the kind of rigor this problem statement 
is trying to encourage: treating the system’s own output as something to be measured and validated, not just 
something to be demoed.
